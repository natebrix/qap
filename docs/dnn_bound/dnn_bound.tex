\vspace{14pt}
{\small\noindent\textbf{Abstract.}\enspace We describe a certified lower bound for the quadratic assignment problem (QAP)
based on the doubly nonnegative (DNN) semidefinite relaxation, and its use in the
branch-and-bound algorithm that solved the nug30 problem in 2000. The bound is the
dual value of an ADMM multiplier whose projection onto the relaxation's face is
negative semidefinite by construction, evaluated by a Gilmore--Lawler computation
on the lifted cost matrix, less explicit floating-point allowances, so that it is
a valid bound after any number of iterations. In the branch-and-bound algorithm
the new bound is computed only near the top of the tree, at nodes that the
quadratic programming bound QPB does not fathom, and is used only to fathom; QPB
continues to supply branching information and to eliminate children. Together with
a new branching rule based on the geometry of grid instances, this solves nug30
in 5,817 nodes and under two hours on a four-core cloud computer, and in 20 minutes
on a laptop, a problem whose
solution in 2000 required $1.19\times10^{10}$ nodes and a week on a computational
grid of hundreds of workstations.

\vspace{8pt}
\noindent\textbf{Key words.}\enspace Quadratic assignment problem -- branch and bound --
semidefinite programming -- doubly nonnegative relaxation\par}

\section{Introduction}

The quadratic assignment problem (QAP) is a standard problem in location theory.
The QAP in ``Koopmans--Beckmann'' form is to
\[
  \min_{\pi}\ \sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{k=1}^n a_{ik}\,b_{\pi(i),\pi(k)} + \sum_{i=1}^n c_{i,\pi(i)},
\]
where $a_{ik}$ is the flow between facilities $i$ and $k$, $b_{jl}$ is the
distance between locations $j$ and $l$, $c_{ij}$ is the fixed cost of assigning
facility $i$ to location $j$, and $\pi(i) = j$ if facility $i$ is assigned to
location $j$. The problem can alternatively be represented in matrix form as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qap}
  \begin{array}{rl}
  \mathrm{QAP}(A,B,C): & \min\ \tr(AXB + C)X^T\\
  & \ \text{s.t.}\ \ X\in\Pi,
  \end{array}
\end{equation}
where $\Pi$ is the set of $n\times n$ permutation matrices and $X_{ij} = 1$ if
facility $i$ is assigned to location $j$. Throughout we assume that at least one of
$A$ and $B$ is symmetric. Replacing the other by its symmetric part then changes no
objective value, so we may, and do, take both to be symmetric. QAPLIB \cite{qaplib}
has long served as the benchmark library for the QAP; for surveys see
\cite{burkardsurvey,loiola}.

The QAP is NP-hard, and in practice has proven to be one of the hardest discrete
optimization problems to solve to optimality. Exact methods are almost exclusively
branch and bound, and their success rests on the lower bound. For implementation in
a branch-and-bound algorithm the relationship between the quality of a bound and
its computational cost is extremely important, as is the quality of the branching
information that bound computations provide. The Gilmore--Lawler bound (GLB)
\cite{gilmore,lawler} is cheap but weak for larger problems; bounds based on linear
programming and its duals, on eigenvalues \cite{hrw}, and on semidefinite
programming (SDP) \cite{zkrw} are progressively stronger and more expensive. A
convex quadratic programming bound QPB \cite{ab01} occupies a useful middle ground:
better than GLB and the projected eigenvalue bound PB \cite{hrw}, at far lower cost
than LP or SDP bounds, and it provides a dual matrix that serves both to eliminate
children and to choose how to branch \cite{ba01}.

The most commonly used benchmark QAPs are the ``Nugent'' problems, introduced in
1968 \cite{nugent}, whose distance matrices are Manhattan distances on rectangular
grids. Their solution has marked advances in both processor capability and QAP
solution methods: nug20 was first solved in 1995, nug21 and nug22 in 1997, and nug25
in 1998, each on a parallel computer with tens to hundreds of processors (see
\cite{abgl} for the history). In June 2000 Anstreicher, Brixius, Goux and Linderoth
\cite{abgl} solved nug30 with a QPB-based branch and bound running on a
computational grid: an average of 653 worker machines over 6.9 days, the
equivalent of almost 7 years of computation on a single HP9000 C3000 workstation,
in a tree of $1.19\times10^{10}$ nodes. The same code then solved kra30b, kra32 and
tho30. Since then, semidefinite relaxations have become practical inside branch and
bound: Fujii et al.\ \cite{fujii} bounded every node of a branch-and-bound tree
with a Lagrangian-DNN relaxation and, on large parallel computers, solved
nug30-sized instances with tens of thousands of nodes as well as several QAPLIB
instances that had remained open.

In this paper we revisit nug30 with the branch-and-bound algorithm of
\cite{ba01,abgl}, on a single computer. Three changes make this possible:
\begin{itemize}
\item[(C1)] a \emph{certified} DNN bound (Section~\ref{sec:dnn}): the doubly
  nonnegative relaxation of Zhao, Karisch, Rendl and Wolkowicz \cite{zkrw}, in the
  facially reduced form of Oliveira, Wolkowicz and Xu \cite{owx} and solved by their
  ADMM, with a lower bound that is valid at every iteration. It is within 3\% of the
  optimum at the root of nug30, against 13\% for QPB, but each evaluation takes
  seconds rather than milliseconds;
\item[(C2)] a new branching rule (Section~\ref{sec:branch}) that branches on the
  \emph{most eccentric free location}, the free location farthest from the others,
  at no cost per node, and a cheaper lookahead rule built on it;
\item[(C3)] a division of labor (Section~\ref{sec:fathom}): the DNN bound is computed
  only at the top of the tree, at nodes that QPB does not fathom, and is used only to
  fathom. QPB continues to supply the bound used for branching and the reduced costs
  used to eliminate children, and does all the work below.
\end{itemize}
With these, nug30 is solved in 5,817 nodes and 1.7 hours on four cores of a cloud
computer, and in 20 minutes on a laptop with eight solver processes
(Section~\ref{sec:results}). In Section~\ref{sec:dnn}
we describe the DNN relaxation, the certificate and its implementation. In
Section~\ref{sec:bnb} we review the branch-and-bound algorithm of \cite{abgl} and
describe the new branching rules and the use of the DNN bound. In
Section~\ref{sec:results} we give bounds for the QAPLIB problems of \cite{ab01},
the effect of the DNN bound on the Nugent problems, and the solution of nug30. The
validation of the bound is described in Appendix~\ref{sec:validation}.

\paragraph{Notation.} We use $\tr(\cdot)$ to denote the trace of a matrix. If $A$
and $B$ are matrices then $\inner{A}{B} = \tr(AB^T)$, $A\otimes B$ is the Kronecker
product of $A$ and $B$, and $\vecop(A)$ is the vector formed by ``stacking'' the
columns of $A$. We use $e$ to denote a vector with each component equal to one,
$\Sym^N$ for the symmetric $N\times N$ matrices, and $\lmax(\cdot)$ for the largest
eigenvalue of a symmetric matrix. For convenience we let the name of an
optimization problem, like $\mathrm{QAP}(A,B,C)$, also refer to its optimal value.

\section{A certified DNN bound}\label{sec:dnn}

\subsection{The DNN relaxation}

Consider a node of the branch-and-bound tree with $m$ facilities left to assign.
Its subproblem is again a QAP \eqref{eq:qap} of size $m$, with reduced matrices
$A, B \in \R^{m\times m}$, a linear term $C$ that collects the interactions with the
fixed assignments, and a constant $s$. For $X\in\Pi$ let $x = \vecop(X^T)$, ordered
so that $x_{ij}$ (facility $i$ at location $j$) has lifted index $1 + im + j$, and
let $y = (1, x)$ and $N = m^2+1$. Define the symmetric lifted cost $L\in\Sym^N$ by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:L}
  L_{(ij),(kl)} = \tfrac12(a_{ik}b_{jl} + a_{ki}b_{lj}), \qquad
  L_{0,(ij)} = L_{(ij),0} = \tfrac12 c_{ij}, \qquad L_{00} = 0.
\end{equation}
Then for every permutation, $\inner{L}{yy^T} = \sum a_{ik}b_{jl}x_{ij}x_{kl} +
\sum c_{ij}x_{ij}$, the subproblem objective without $s$, for any $A$ and $B$,
symmetric or not.

\paragraph{The face.} The constraints $Xe = X^Te = e$ say that $y$ lies in the null
space of a linear map, so every lifted permutation $Y = yy^T$ has its range in a
fixed subspace of dimension $(m-1)^2+1$. Let $V \in\R^{N\times K}$, $K = (m-1)^2+1$,
be an orthonormal basis of it. Zhao et al.\ \cite{zkrw} take
$V = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ e/m & V_m\otimes V_m\end{pmatrix}$, suitably normalized,
where the columns of $V_m\in\R^{m\times(m-1)}$ span $e^\perp$. Every lifted
permutation then satisfies $Y = VRV^T$ with $R \succeq 0$. The DNN relaxation of the
subproblem is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dnn}
  \begin{array}{rl}
  \DNN(A,B,C) = s + \min & \inner{L}{Y}\\
  \text{s.t.} & Y = VRV^T,\ R\succeq 0,\ Y_{00}=1,\ \mathcal{G}(Y) = 0,\ 0 \le Y \le 1,
  \end{array}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{G}$ selects the ``gangster'' entries $Y_{(ij),(il)}$, $l\ne j$, and
$Y_{(ij),(kj)}$, $k\ne i$, which vanish for permutations since a facility has one
location and a location one facility. Oliveira, Wolkowicz and Xu \cite{owx} solve
\eqref{eq:dnn} by ADMM on the splitting $Y = VRV^T$, alternating
\begin{align}
  R &\leftarrow \Pspd\big(V^T(Y + Z/\beta)V\big), \label{eq:rstep}\\
  Y &\leftarrow \Pi_{\mathcal{Y}}\big(VRV^T - (L + Z)/\beta\big), \label{eq:ystep}\\
  Z &\leftarrow Z + \gamma\beta\,(Y - VRV^T), \label{eq:zstep}
\end{align}
where $\Pspd$ projects onto the positive semidefinite cone,
$\mathcal{Y} = \{Y: Y_{00}=1, \mathcal{G}(Y)=0, 0\le Y\le1\}$ is a box with fixed
entries (so $\Pi_{\mathcal{Y}}$ is entrywise clipping), $\beta>0$ is the penalty
and $\gamma\in(0,\frac{1+\sqrt5}{2})$ the dual step.

\subsection{A bound from any multiplier}\label{sec:cert}

ADMM iterates are neither primal feasible nor dual optimal, and the primal value
$\inner{L}{Y}$ is not a bound: stopped early, it can lie above the optimum.
Branch and bound needs a number that is provably below the subproblem optimum after
any number of iterations. We obtain one from the multiplier.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:trace}
Every lifted permutation $Y = yy^T$ satisfies $Y = VRV^T$ with
$R = V^TYV\succeq0$ and $\tr(R) = m+1$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Since $y$ lies in the range of $V$,
\[
  Y = VV^TYVV^T = VRV^T, \qquad R = V^TYV = (V^Ty)(V^Ty)^T\succeq0,
\]
and $\tr R = \|V^Ty\|^2 = \|y\|^2 = 1 + \sum x_{ij}^2 = m+1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:cert}
Let $Z\in\Sym^N$ be any symmetric matrix, $\lambda \ge \lmax(V^TZV)$, and let
$\mathcal{P}\supseteq\{yy^T : X\in\Pi\}$. Then the subproblem optimum is at least
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cert}
  s + \min_{Y\in\mathcal{P}} \inner{L+Z}{Y} - (m+1)\lambda .
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For a lifted permutation $Y = VRV^T$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:trace}), since $R\succeq0$,
\[
  \inner{Z}{Y} = \inner{V^TZV}{R} \le \lmax(V^TZV)\tr R \le (m+1)\lambda .
\]
Hence
\begin{align*}
  \inner{L}{Y} = \inner{L+Z}{Y} - \inner{Z}{Y} &\ge \inner{L+Z}{Y} - (m+1)\lambda\\
  &\ge \min_{\mathcal{P}}\inner{L+Z}{\cdot} - (m+1)\lambda. \qedhere
\end{align*}
\end{proof}

The same bound appears as the dual function of Graham et al.\ \cite[eq.~(3.11),
Thm.~3.4]{graham}. Proposition~\ref{prop:cert} holds for every $Z$; the quality of
the bound depends on $Z$ being near a dual optimum of \eqref{eq:dnn}, and on
choosing $Z$ so that $\lambda$ can be small. Two choices of $\mathcal{P}$ and one of
$Z$ make it both cheap and tight.

\paragraph{The multiplier.} The prototype of \cite{owx} projects $V^TZV$ onto the
negative semidefinite cone, which costs an extra eigendecomposition per check.
Instead we use the multiplier implied by the $R$-step. Let $W = V^T(Y+Z/\beta)V$ be
the matrix projected in \eqref{eq:rstep}, and
\[
  Z' = Z + \beta(Y - VRV^T), \qquad\text{so that}\qquad V^TZ'V = \beta(W - \Pspd(W)) \preceq 0 .
\]
The last matrix is $\beta$ times the negative part of $W$, negative semidefinite by
construction. This holds even when the eigenvectors used for $\Pspd$ are
inaccurate: if $\Pspd(W)$ is formed as $W$ minus the computed negative part
$\sum_{w_k<0} w_k v_kv_k^T$, the difference is exactly that sum. In floating
point, $\lmax(V^TZ'V)$ is a tiny positive number, which we bound below.

\paragraph{The polyhedral part.} With $\mathcal{P}$ the box $\mathcal{Y}$ itself,
$\min_{\mathcal{P}}\inner{M}{Y}$ for $M = L+Z'$ is $M_{00}$ plus the sum of the
negative entries of $M$ outside the gangster set: the prototype's choice. A much
tighter, still cheap choice uses that every permutation is an assignment in two
levels. Write $M_{(ij),\cdot}$ for row $(ij)$ of $M$. For a permutation $\sigma$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gl}
\inner{M}{yy^T} = M_{00} + \sum_{i}\Big( 2M_{0,(i\sigma_i)} + M_{(i\sigma_i),(i\sigma_i)}
 + \sum_{k\ne i} M_{(i\sigma_i),(k\sigma_k)} \Big).
\end{equation}
The inner sum is a perfect matching between facilities $k\ne i$ and locations
$l\ne\sigma_i$, so it is at least the value $\LAP_{ij}$ of the $(m-1)\times(m-1)$
assignment problem on the block $M_{(ij),(kl)}$, $k\ne i$, $l\ne j$. Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:glbound}
\min_{\sigma}\inner{M}{yy^T} \;\ge\; \GL(M) := M_{00} + \LAP\big(c\big),\qquad
c_{ij} = 2M_{0,(ij)} + M_{(ij),(ij)} + \LAP_{ij},
\end{equation}
a Gilmore--Lawler computation \cite{gilmore,lawler} on the lifted matrix. It also
bounds the DNN relaxation itself, not only permutations: for feasible $Y$ of
\eqref{eq:dnn}, column $(ij)$ restricted to $k\ne i$, $l\ne j$ is nonnegative with
all row and column sums $x_{ij} = Y_{0,(ij)}$, so it is $x_{ij}$ times a doubly
stochastic matrix, and assignment duality applies twice. It is never below the box
value and reaches a given value 1.5--6$\times$ sooner in the nug20 tests. Any lower
bound on each $\LAP_{ij}$ may replace it, for example the row-then-column
reduction, which costs $O(m^2)$. The implementation uses the reduction, plus exact
Hungarian solves when the result is within a small gap of the cutoff.

\begin{remark}
Nothing in the certificate depends on the ADMM having converged, on the accuracy
of its eigenvectors, or on the single-precision arithmetic used in the $R$-step
(Section~\ref{sec:impl}). Only the multiplier $Z'$, the Cholesky test below and
\eqref{eq:glbound} enter the certified value, all in double precision.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Floating-point safety}\label{sec:fp}

We assume IEEE binary64 arithmetic with rounding to nearest, an extended type
(\texttt{long double}) with a $p$-bit significand ($p=64$ on x86), and no overflow
or underflow; a non-finite value anywhere yields no certificate. The certified
value is $s + \GL(L+Z') - \mu$, rounded down, where the inputs and the margin $\mu$
cover:
\begin{itemize}
\item[(o)] the subproblem data: $s$ and the reduced linear term $C$, sums of
  products that can cancel, are replaced by lower bounds (exact when all operands
  are multiples of $\frac12$ and the sum of magnitudes is below $2^{p-2}$, as for
  integer data; otherwise the extended-precision sum less $(k+2)\,2^{-p}\sum|t|$
  for $k$ terms $t$, rounded down). Bounds for the lowered subproblem are bounds for
  the exact one, since assignment variables are nonnegative;
\item[(i)] $(m+1)\lambda$ with $\lambda\ge\lmax(V^TZ'V)$, obtained by Rump's
  criterion \cite{rump}: if the floating-point Cholesky factorization of
  $\delta I - S$ succeeds for the computed $S \approx V^TZ'V$, then
  $\lmax(S) \le \delta + c$ with an explicit $c$ depending on the dimension, the
  unit roundoff $u$ and $\tr(\delta I - S)$. To this is added
  $64\,m\,u\,\|Z'\|_F$ for the error of computing $S$ through Householder
  transforms rather than with an exact $V$;
\item[(ii)] the summation and rounding errors of \eqref{eq:glbound}, accumulated in
  extended precision: $8(m+2)u\,\|M\|_1$, plus $(m+2)u\sum_{(ij),(kl)}
  \frac12(|a_{ik}b_{jl}|+|a_{ki}b_{lj}|)$ for forming and scaling $L$.
\end{itemize}
These are standard error bounds with generous constants, not interval arithmetic,
and not every constant is derived here: the allowance $64\,m\,u\|Z'\|_F$ for the
Householder transforms rests on a standard backward-error argument and was checked
empirically (errors at most $2.1\,u\|Z'\|_F$), not proved. At $m=28$ the largest
margin seen was about $10^{-7}$ in objective units, against integer objective
values.

\subsection{Implementation}\label{sec:impl}

The bound is implemented in C++ (\texttt{latest/dnn.C}, about 1200 lines) with
LAPACK and BLAS (OpenBLAS). A numpy prototype (\texttt{experiments/dnn\_bound}) was
ported faithfully; a prototype mode reproduces its certified values to $10^{-7}$.

\paragraph{Products with $V$.} The prototype forms $V$ explicitly and computes
$V^TMV$ and $VRV^T$ with dense products of order $N\times N$, which costs
$O(m^6)$. We take for $V_m$ the first $m-1$ columns of the Householder reflector
$H$ with $He_{m-1} = e/\sqrt m$. Then $V^TMV$ and $VRV^T$ are a reflection along
each of the four tensor modes plus a $2\times2$ rotation coupling the constant
coordinate, $O(m^4)$ per product. Both the $R$-step and the certificate are
invariant under an orthogonal change of basis of the face, so any orthonormal $V$
gives the same iterates and bound.

\paragraph{Eigensolver.} Each $R$-step needs the projection of a $K\times K$
symmetric matrix ($K = 730$ at $m=28$) with hundreds of eigenvalues of each sign,
so a partial spectrum does not help directly. Between iterations the matrix changes
little. We track its negative eigenspace by a Chebyshev-filtered subspace iteration
in single precision (BLAS level 3), with a full single-precision eigendecomposition
every 20 iterations. Because $R$ is formed as $W$ minus the computed negative part,
the certificate is unaffected. When a check fails the Cholesky test, or the
semidefinite penalty alone decides whether the node is fathomed, the multiplier is
re-projected in double precision before certifying.

\paragraph{Parameters.} The objective is scaled to $\max|L| = 1$. We use
$\beta = 0.3\cdot m/3$ (the prototype's $m/3$ times 0.3), which certifies
2--3$\times$ sooner on hard nodes, and $\gamma = 1.618$. A certificate is computed
every 10 iterations, and the run stops at the first certified value above the
cutoff. An optional give-up rule stops a node early when its certified value,
extrapolated generously from its recent gains, stays below the cutoff. This only
affects cost: the node is then branched on as usual.

\paragraph{Warm starts.} When the DNN bound at a node misses, its children can start
their ADMM from its final multiplier, with the row and column of the new assignment
removed from the lifted indices. Only $Z$ is carried over; starting $Y$ from the
parent made children slower. This cuts the iterations to fathom a child by about
40\%, but less of the time, because a warm-started ADMM pays for many negative
eigenvalues from its first iteration where a cold one starts cheap.

\paragraph{Speed.} Table~\ref{tab:speed} compares the C++ bound with the prototype
on the same nodes. Measured back to back on a loaded 4-core Xeon, an ADMM iteration
at $m=28$ costs 43\,ms against 339\,ms. Per node, the C++ bound needs fewer
iterations to certify, thanks to the tighter polyhedral part and the smaller
$\beta$, and is 13--36$\times$ faster.

\section{The branch-and-bound algorithm}\label{sec:bnb}

In this section we review the branch-and-bound algorithm of \cite{ba01,abgl},
describe the changes made to it since 2000, including a new branching rule, and
describe how the DNN bound is used.

\subsection{QPB, elimination and branching}

The quadratic programming bound of \cite{ab01} is
\[
  \QPB(A,B,C):\quad \min\ \vecop(X)^TQ\,\vecop(X) + \inner{C}{X} + \gamma
  \quad\text{s.t.}\quad Xe = X^Te = e,\ X\ge0,
\]
where $Q = (B\otimes A) - (I\otimes S) - (T\otimes I)$, and $S$, $T$ and $\gamma$
come from the spectral decompositions of $V^TAV$ and $V^TBV$; $Q$ is positive
semidefinite on the null space of the equality constraints, and
$\mathrm{PB}(A,B,C)\le\QPB(A,B,C)\le\mathrm{QAP}(A,B,C)$. As in \cite{ba01,abgl} we
compute it approximately by the Frank--Wolfe algorithm, which is known to have poor
asymptotic performance, but in the context of QPB is attractive because each
iteration is dominated by a single matrix multiplication and the solution of a
dense linear assignment problem. It produces a lower bound $z$ and a dual matrix
$U\ge0$ such that $\tr(AXB+C)X^T \ge z + \inner{U}{X}$ for every permutation matrix
$X$. If $v$ is the incumbent value, then $z + U_{ij} > v$ implies that $X_{ij} = 0$ in
any improving solution.

Branching is ``polytomic'': child problems are created either by assigning a fixed
facility to all available locations (\emph{row branching}) or by assigning all
available facilities to a fixed location (\emph{column branching}), and logic based
on $U$ eliminates children in both cases. Where the symmetry of a grid instance can
be exploited, only row branching on restricted location sets is used. The rules of
\cite{abgl} used here are:
\begin{itemize}
\item[] \textbf{Rule 2.} Branch on the row or column that produces the smallest
  number of children, breaking ties by the largest sum of the remaining $U_{ij}$.
\item[] \textbf{Rule 4.} For the candidate rows and columns with the largest $U$
  sums, bound every child by QPB and branch on the line whose children score best,
  using their bounds and their own dual matrices. Rule 4 is a \emph{strong
  branching}, or \emph{look-ahead}, rule.
\end{itemize}
A rule is chosen at each node from a table of strategies by the node's depth and
its relative gap $g = (v - z')/(v - z_0)$, where $z'$ is the bound inherited from
the parent and $z_0$ the root bound: the strategies are scanned from the most
expensive to the least until one is found whose gap tolerance is below $g$ and
whose depth limit is at least the node's depth.

\paragraph{Changes since 2000.} Several changes improve QPB's efficiency without
changing what it computes. Each node's Frank--Wolfe iterations start from its
parent's solution, with the fixed row and column removed, instead of from the
uniform matrix, and the bound kept is the best (bound, $U$) pair over all
iterations rather than the last; on nug24 this alone saves 13\% of the time. A bug
in the code of 2000 made Rule 4 rank its candidate lines essentially arbitrarily;
corrected, it saves 4\% on our standard test set. Rule 4's child bounds now use fewer
Frank--Wolfe iterations (NFW3 of 25 at depths 1--2 and 12 below), starting from
the parent's solution; this saves 24\% on the standard set. On nug25, with the
optimum as the incumbent, the resulting code takes 52,989,998 nodes and 18,353 CPU
seconds, against $7.1\times10^7$ nodes and $1.1\times10^6$ CPU seconds on the
processors of 2000 \cite[Table~3]{abgl}.

\subsection{Branching on eccentric locations}\label{sec:branch}

On grid instances, locations differ in a way the dual matrix $U$ does not see. A
facility at a corner or an edge of the grid is far from many others, so fixing a
facility there creates children with large, spread-out linear terms, and more of
them fathom. We define the \emph{eccentricity} of a free location $j$ as the
largest distance from $j$ to another free location,
$\max\{b_{jl} : l \ne j \text{ free}\}$.

\begin{itemize}
\item[] \textbf{Rule 6.} Branch on the column (location) of largest eccentricity,
  breaking ties by the number of children eliminated and then by the $U$ sum, as in
  Rule 2. If all free locations are equally eccentric, use Rule 2.
\item[] \textbf{Rule 7.} Rule 4 restricted to a smaller candidate set: the
  NBEST most eccentric columns and the NBEST$-1$ rows with the largest $U$ sums.
\end{itemize}

Rule 6 costs nothing per node, unlike lookahead, which bounds every child of every
candidate line. Used instead of lookahead at depths 3--4, it makes dense instances
much faster (nug20 2.1$\times$, nug21 1.7$\times$, tai17a 2.0$\times$, 1.3$\times$
on our standard test set), but sparse ones, where lookahead pays for itself, slower (chr15a 1.4$\times$ slower, esc16a more than
2.9$\times$ slower; esc16a's hypercube distances make every location equally
eccentric). Rule 7 keeps lookahead where it pays and bounds fewer lines: against
Rule 4 with five rows and five columns, it takes 0.80 of the time on nug20, 0.87 on
nug21, 0.79 on tai17a and 0.69 on esc16a, and 1.13 on chr15a, a geometric mean of
0.84. The rows keep it working where locations do not differ. Table~\ref{tab:strategy}
gives the strategy used for the grid instances in this paper, with Rule 7 at
depths 3--4 and Rule 6 down to depth 6. The 2000 solve of nug30 used Rule 4 down to
depth 7 and Rule 2 below, with gap thresholds from 0.34 to 0.03
\cite[Table~5.11]{thesis}. On nug25, with QPB alone, it takes
40,183,792 nodes and 12,362 CPU seconds, against 52,989,998 nodes and 18,353 seconds
for the default strategy (Rule 7 at depths 3--4 and Rule 2 below).

\subsection{Using the DNN bound to fathom}\label{sec:fathom}

The DNN bound is added to the algorithm as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
\item At a node of depth $d\in[d_1,d_2]$ whose QPB value does not fathom it, and
  whose relative gap is at most a given $g_{\max}$, the certified DNN bound is
  computed for its reduced subproblem (\texttt{bnbdrv -x
  d1:d2[:gap[:giveup[:warm]]]}).
\item If it exceeds the incumbent minus the fathoming tolerance, the node is
  fathomed. Otherwise it raises the node's recorded bound, which its children
  inherit and which also feeds the choice of strategy and some branching scores.
\item Branching, and the elimination of children by $z + U_{ij} > v$, use the QPB
  value and its dual matrix $U$, never the DNN value. The elimination argument is a
  property of the Frank--Wolfe linearization of QPB, and holds only with the bound
  it is paired with.
\end{enumerate}

Near the top of the tree nodes are few, and each carries a subtree of $10^3$ to
$10^9$ QPB nodes, so a bound that costs seconds pays for itself many times over
when it fathoms. Deep in the tree it would not. Combining bounds is not new in
itself: earlier experiments that mixed QPB with GLB \cite{thesis} switched the bound
wholesale, using one bound for both bounding and branching at a node, and did not
pay off, both because GLB is weak even deep in the tree and because each bound was
then doing the job it is worse at. Here each bound is pointed at the job it does
well. Nor does the DNN bound provide
useful branching information cheaply: the outer assignment problem of
\eqref{eq:glbound} yields, with its dual potentials $u$ and $v$, a valid bound
$\GL(M) + r_{ij}$ for every child $x_{ij}=1$ of a node the bound misses, with
$r_{ij} = c_{ij}-u_i-v_j$, at no extra cost. On nine sampled depth-1 and depth-2
misses of nug30 it fathomed none of their 784--841 children each: near a dual
optimum the outer costs are almost flat (median $r_{ij}$ 0.1, largest 12.1, where the
nearest miss needed 14.4), and even forcing the assignment in the outer problem gains
at most 12.1. A child's gain comes from re-solving the relaxation with the extra
fixing.

\paragraph{Where to apply it.} Two studies with the prototype measured the bound on
QPB-open nodes (Table~\ref{tab:fathom}). At depth 1 it rarely fathoms. At depth 2 it
fathoms nearly all nodes of nug20, and 34 of a random sample of 36 of nug30's 225
(94\%). In that sample the outcome was almost a function of the node's QPB value:
every node with QPB below about 5548 was missed, every node above about 5554
fathomed. Applied to the whole depth-2 frontier, that threshold predicts about 185
of 225 fathoms (82\%), a sample-based estimate rather than a guarantee for other
depths or instances. At depth 3, under the depth-2 nodes it misses, it fathoms
almost everything. The misses are the most expensive subtrees, so the bound has to
be applied at several consecutive depths. In the full solves, depths 2 to 6 or 7
worked best.

The two bounds share a lineage: QPB is built on the projected eigenvalue bound PB
\cite{hrw}, and the DNN relaxation on the same projection onto the assignment
constraints. To see whether that is why QPB predicts the DNN outcome, we computed
GLB, PB and QPB at all 225 depth-2 nodes of nug30 and ran the DNN bound on each
(165 are fathomed within 1,000 iterations, the same count as in the full solves).
Measured by the area under the ROC curve for separating fathomed from missed
nodes, QPB is the best predictor (0.990, and 0.994 for its parametric form), the
lookahead estimate the solver already has before it bounds the node is close
behind (0.988), then GLB (0.969) and PB (0.920). At its best threshold QPB classifies
96.9\% of the nodes correctly, GLB 93.8\% and PB 86.2\%; with QPB, fathomed and
missed nodes overlap only in a band of about 25 units around 5590. So the purely
spectral PB, from which QPB descends, is the weakest predictor, and the purely
combinatorial GLB, a far weaker bound, is a good one. QPB combines a spectral part
with assignment problems, much as the DNN certificate combines a semidefinite part
with Gilmore--Lawler assignment problems, and it is that combination, more than the
spectral lineage alone, that makes the cheap bound a good guide to the expensive
one. (One instance at one depth: suggestive rather than conclusive.)

Does it follow that branching choices made with QPB also serve the DNN bound? We
took five depth-2 nodes the DNN bound misses and, for nine candidate lines at each
(four rows, four columns and the most eccentric free location), bounded all 1,260
children by QPB and by the DNN bound with the solver's settings. At the two
near-miss nodes every child of every line is fathomed, so the choice does not
matter. At the farthest miss the lines give 6 to 17 child misses, and QPB ranks
them well: the rank correlation between misses and the average child QPB is 0.92,
and 0.98 for the number of children with QPB above the level's threshold. In
between the ranking is noisy, and the average child QPB, Rule 4's score, sometimes
orders the lines backwards. The most eccentric location's line was as good as the
best line at four of the five nodes and one child miss worse at the fifth. So where
the choice matters QPB-based branching picks good lines for the DNN bound, and
Rule 6 does as well at no cost; but most DNN misses are near misses, where it does
not matter, which is why more lookahead at these depths changes little.

\paragraph{Fathoming tolerance.} The fathoming tolerance is 1.95 when all objective
values are provably even, as for the nug instances, 0.95 when they are integers, and
0 otherwise; data is treated as integer only if every objective value is below
$2^{53}$, so that it is computed exactly. For even values and an even incumbent, an
improving solution is at most the incumbent minus 2, so 1.95 leaves a cushion of
0.05 for rounding.

\paragraph{Scope.} The DNN certificate covers only the DNN prunes. QPB values, QPB
reduced-cost eliminations and symmetry reductions are computed in floating point
without such margins, as in \cite{abgl}, so the search as a whole is not an
independently checkable proof. The full solver also needs one of $A$, $B$ to be
symmetric (otherwise QPB is not valid, and the driver warns), and its QPB search is
currently wrong on data with negative entries in $A$ or $B$
(Appendix~\ref{sec:validation}). The DNN bound has neither restriction.

\paragraph{Parallel implementation.} The 2000 solve used the master-worker library
MW on the Condor system to distribute subtrees to hundreds of workers. Here a small
driver (\texttt{bench/parallel.py}) does the same on one machine: the root is
solved serially, its children go into a pool, and one solver process per core each
take a chunk of nodes, returning their unfinished nodes when idle workers need work.
Search statistics, including DNN counts, are kept in checkpoint files, so a run can
be stopped, resumed and split across processes with identical totals. The DNN
bound's floating-point results depend on the BLAS kernels selected for the CPU, so
node counts can differ between machines if a certified value lands within about
$10^{-6}$ of a cutoff. Validity is not affected.

\section{Computational results}\label{sec:results}

All runs use the known optimum as the incumbent, so they measure the work of the
proof; the runs of \cite{abgl} started from the best known value plus 2 and so also
had to recover an optimal solution. They ran on cloud virtual machines with 4 cores
(Intel Xeon, 2.1--2.8\,GHz), with four solver processes, and on an Apple M2 Pro
laptop (6 performance and 4 efficiency cores), with eight solver processes and
Apple's Accelerate library for BLAS and LAPACK. CPU is the sum over
processes.

\subsection{The solution of nug30}

nug30 was solved three times. The first solve applied the DNN bound at depths 2--5.
QPB search below the depth-5 nodes the bound missed grew to about 12,000 CPU
seconds, so for the remaining work (506 open nodes, including a whole depth-1
subtree) the bound was extended to depth 6, which finished in 21 minutes. In total,
45,486,332 nodes and 40,555 CPU seconds; the DNN bound ran on 5,615 nodes, fathomed
5,403, and accounted for 26,770 of those seconds. A run with the DNN bound at depths
2--6 throughout took \CleanNodes{} nodes, \CleanCPU{} CPU seconds and \CleanWall{}
of wall time: the DNN bound ran on 5,764 nodes, fathomed 5,551, and accounted for
17,192 CPU seconds, 92\% of the total. Almost all of the difference from the first
solve is QPB search below depth-6 nodes that the depth-5 setting left to QPB.

Two changes target that run's remaining costs. The QPB search below its 72 depth-7
nodes took about 1,300 CPU seconds; applying the DNN bound at depth 7 too fathoms all
of them. And most DNN calls are at children of nodes whose own DNN bound missed,
which can be warm-started. The run with both (\texttt{-x 2:7:1:300:1}) ran on a
different machine, so the depth-6 settings were rerun there with the same code (the
last two rows of Table~\ref{tab:bnb}). The rerun reproduces the earlier tree exactly,
and the new settings take 17\% less CPU, with 5,817 nodes in all: every node at
depths 2--7 that QPB does not fathom gets the DNN bound, and all of them are
fathomed by depth 7. Warm starts lower the DNN time per node at depths 3 and 4 by
16\% and 13\%.

The same settings on the laptop solve nug30 in 19.7 minutes. Each DNN node takes
about a third of the time it takes on the cloud Xeon (1.9 against 5.8 seconds at
depth 3, 1.2 against 3.5 at depth 4), and eight solver processes give 7.1 CPU seconds
per second of wall time. The DNN part of the tree is within 0.5\% of the Xeon runs'.
The larger node count comes from two of the 96 depth-7 nodes, which the Xeon runs
fathomed and this run did not: the QPB search below them took 580,000 nodes but only
84 CPU seconds. Which nodes are warm-started depends on the scheduling, and
Accelerate rounds differently from OpenBLAS; either can change when a node is given
up as hopeless. Without the give-up rule and warm starts (\texttt{-x 2:5}), the
laptop reproduces the Xeon trees of nug27 and nug28 exactly.

\subsection{Open problems}

We screened candidates among the instances QAPLIB lists as open by their certified
root DNN gap (nug30: 2.9\%, and tho30, solved in \cite{abgl}: 4.2\%): wil50 1.4\%,
sko49 3.1\%, tho40 5.8\%, tai50b 6.3\%, tai35a 8.5\%, tai40a 9.4\%. At depth 2, 7 of 10
random nug30 nodes are fathomed, but none of 10 for tho40 and none of the sko49 nodes
sampled, whose gap is still about 2\%. At $n\approx50$ an ADMM iteration costs about
1 second on four threads, 13$\times$ nug30's, and the bound would have to reach depth
3--4 before fathoming, which means $10^4$--$10^6$ DNN nodes. The open grid instances
are out of reach on one small machine with this method; they would need much larger
parallel resources, as in \cite{fujii}, or a faster DNN solver.

\section{Conclusion}

Three advances made it possible to solve nug30 on a single small computer: a
certified DNN bound, valid after any number of iterations and cheap enough to stop
as soon as it fathoms; a branching rule that uses the geometry of grid instances at
no cost; and a division of labor in which the strong bound is spent only where a
single fathom removes a large subtree, and QPB, with its branching information and
its eliminations, does the rest. Twenty-six years ago nug30 needed QPB's cheapness:
$1.19\times10^{10}$ nodes, each bounded in milliseconds, on hundreds of machines.
Today the same search needs fewer than six thousand much stronger bounds, and
finishes in 20 minutes on a laptop. In 2000 the computation took 6.9 years of
HP9000 C3000 CPU time; on a cloud Xeon it now takes about $2.4\times10^4$ seconds,
roughly 9,000 times less, and on the laptop about 8,400. A rough breakdown: per-node times of QPB deep in the tree, where
little else has changed, suggest that a current core is about 20 times faster than
a C3000 on this code; the QPB branch and bound itself is about 3 times faster (on
nug25 with QPB alone, 12,400 seconds against 720,000 C3000-seconds in 2000 \cite{thesis}),
from fewer nodes and cheaper Frank--Wolfe iterations; and the DNN bound accounts for
the remaining factor of about 150. Hardware accounts for part of the gain, but most
of it comes from the bound and from where it is spent.

Several directions remain. The give-up rule and the depths of application were
tuned by hand. A QPB threshold predicts the DNN outcome almost perfectly in the
samples, which suggests skipping nodes the bound will miss. The restricted
Peaceman--Rachford method of \cite{graham} may converge faster than ADMM, which
matters most for the larger open grid instances, and a separate verified check of
each fathoming certificate would separate fast search from rigorous verification.

\appendix
\section{Validation}\label{sec:validation}

A branch-and-bound proof is only as good as its bounds, so the DNN bound was
checked in several independent ways.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Exhaustive checks.} 1,512 certified bounds on random instances and
  subproblems with $m\le8$ (several families, including linear terms and ties),
  each compared with the optimum found by enumeration. No bound exceeds the
  optimum, and no fathom is ever claimed with the cutoff at the optimum.
\item \textbf{Inside the search.} The solver's brute-force test (4,536 runs on
  random instances with $n\le 8$, eight parameter sets, three upper-bound modes)
  passes with the DNN bound applied at every depth, with and without the give-up
  rule.
\item \textbf{Against the prototype.} In prototype mode the C++ bound reproduces the
  numpy prototype's certified values at 44 checkpoints to a relative $8.6\cdot10^{-8}$.
  On nug20 nodes whose subproblems were solved exactly, the bounds are below the
  optima and fathom the same nodes.
\item \textbf{Reproducibility.} Results are bitwise identical across runs on one
  machine. Valgrind reports no memory errors on a debug build. Serial, resumed and
  parallel runs give identical node counts.
\item \textbf{Independent mathematical review.} A separate AI agent, given the code
  and this claim but not our arguments, re-derived every step: the face basis
  actually used (Householder reflector and rotation) has the required range, so
  Lemma~\ref{lem:trace} holds for it; Proposition~\ref{prop:cert} holds for any
  sign of $\lmax$; the Gilmore--Lawler evaluation counts every quadratic term
  once and is valid for any dual potentials, so neither the inexact inner LAPs
  nor the outer Hungarian solve need to be exact; the lifting matches the
  solver's objective convention without symmetry assumptions; a NaN or infinite
  multiplier cannot produce a fathom; and the search uses the DNN value only to
  fathom. It found no proof-breaking issue.
\item \textbf{Independent code review and stress tests.} A second agent reviewed
  the implementation and ran 2,592 bounds on brute-forced instances with
  $m\le7$ (asymmetric and negative data, ties, scales up to $10^6$), 540
  warm-started children, an independent long-double check of the face
  transforms (error at most $2.1\,u\|Z\|_F$ against the $64\,m\,u\|Z\|_F$
  allowed), 21,000 random assignment problems against the Hungarian solver,
  and determinism tests, under address, undefined-behavior and leak sanitizers.
  No bound exceeded an optimum and nothing crashed. Its minor findings (and the
  mathematical review's) were fixed: a loop that could skip the last Cholesky
  attempt, widened error constants, an explicit finiteness test on the
  certified value, and skipping the bound while there is no incumbent.
\item \textbf{Adversarial tests.} A third agent tried to break the bound with
  extreme data: magnitudes up to $10^{9}$ and beyond, products above $2^{53}$,
  signed, non-integer and non-symmetric data, rank-one, constant and all-zero
  matrices, subproblems with one to three free facilities, and parameter
  fuzzing; about 15,000 bounds and 8,600 solver runs in all, with an
  instrumented build checking every DNN call inside the search against the
  exact subproblem optimum. On ordinary data no bound was invalid, and the
  rounding part of the margin was never needed (the unmargined value minus the
  semidefinite penalty stayed below the optimum by at least 1.6\% of the rounding
  margin). It did find two ways to exceed the optimum, both needing data that is
  neither symmetric nor small. When neither $A$ nor $B$ is symmetric, an entry of
  $L$ can be the difference of two large products whose rounding errors the
  margin, which scaled with $\sum|L|$, did not cover; the margin now uses the sum
  of the products' magnitudes. And the shift and linear term of a subproblem, sums
  of products that can cancel, were rounded in double before any bound saw them.
  Integer data whose products exceed $2^{53}$ is no longer given an integer
  fathoming tolerance. It also found
  two older bugs outside the DNN bound: a finite sentinel ($10^8$) in the
  assignment solver, which crashed or gave invalid QPB bounds when costs exceeded
  it (fixed), and wrong QPB results on data with negative entries in $A$ or $B$
  (open; QAPLIB data are nonnegative). None of this affects nonnegative integer
  data whose objective values stay below $2^{53}$, where every product and sum is
  exact. That includes all of QAPLIB, even the bur26 family, in which neither
  matrix is symmetric. On the regression instances, node counts are unchanged.
\item \textbf{External review.} An independent review of an earlier draft and the
  code (Astra, an OpenAI Codex agent) re-derived the bound and confirmed that QPB
  values stay paired with their own reduced costs. It showed that accumulating the
  shift and linear terms in extended precision, our first repair, is not enough:
  with $ab = 2^{64}+2^{34}+3$ and $c = -(2^{64}+2^{34})$ the extended-precision
  product rounds by 1 before cancelling, and both the $m=1$ path and a reduced
  subproblem fathomed at a cutoff equal to the optimum. These data now get the
  lower bounds of item (o) in Section~\ref{sec:fp}; both cases, and the
  adversarial ones above, are regression tests (\texttt{tests/dnn\_test.py}, part D)
  that check the bound against optima computed in exact rational arithmetic.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Limitations.} The certificate is a theorem in exact arithmetic
(Proposition~\ref{prop:cert} and \eqref{eq:glbound}). In floating point it rests
on the margins of Section~\ref{sec:fp}: standard error bounds with generous
constants under the stated arithmetic model, not interval arithmetic, and not
every constant is derived. The validation above is strong regression evidence, not
a formal verification. For the nug instances the data are small integers, so the
subproblem data are exact, and a false fathom would need a certified value above
the optimum by more than the 0.05 cushion, five to six orders of magnitude above
any margin observed. For non-integer data, where the tolerance is zero, validity
rests on the margins alone; a verified evaluation (directed rounding or interval
arithmetic) of each fathoming certificate would be the safe choice. Beyond the DNN
bound, the QPB part of the search is trusted floating point, as it was in 2000.
